There is a question that tends to appear just after I close a story: “Yes, it works. But what actually happened here?”

It is an inconvenient question. That is why I like it.

I have been building software for long enough to know that finishing a story and understanding everything that happened around it are not necessarily the same thing. Work has to be scoped, delivered, verified, and moved on from. That discipline keeps a small fix from turning into an expedition across three system layers, two specifications, and an architectural decision that looked obvious until it did not.

It is not the ticket's fault that I still want to pull at the thread.

## What does not fit in the story

Some questions resolve themselves while I work. Others stop being necessary before they are answered.

The query now responds on time, but I still want to know what was blocking it. The architectural decision has been made, although a rejected alternative is beginning to look less absurd. The agent passes its test with controlled data and then encounters real data, that hostile environment where certainties acquire nuance with admirable efficiency.

None of this prevents the work from being closed. None of it vanishes when it is.

Sometimes a provisional explanation is enough. Sometimes I put the question aside because chasing it at that moment would be irresponsible. And sometimes, when I return without the pressure of delivery, I discover that I was asking the wrong question.

At this point I usually have two options: accept that it works and get on with my life, or prepare a minimal test to find out why.

I do not always choose the sensible option.

<Observation title="OBSERVATION">
  A question becoming non-blocking does not make it uninteresting.
</Observation>

## A second context

Out of Context comes from that difference between what a story needs and what my curiosity is still asking for.

The question first appeared inside a specific context: an incident, a feature, an architecture review, or an experiment. Then it fell outside the scope. Not because it was irrelevant, but because answering it was no longer required to deliver the work well.

Here it gets a second context.

Not to turn every detour into a solemn investigation or every conclusion into a universal law. I want to preserve the path: what made me stop, what I thought was happening, what I tested, what went wrong, and what I eventually changed. Sometimes that will require code. Other times it will be a measurement, a short postmortem, or a decision that only makes sense beside the alternative I rejected.

The story may begin in .NET, a database, an interface, or an agent. That is almost incidental. Technology provides the setting; the question decides whether what happened is worth telling.

## What remains afterwards

I do not expect every exploration to end with a neat answer. Some will leave me with a rule I will apply from then on. Others will document a mistake and stop my future self from presenting it as a new idea. The most interesting ones may simply replace a comfortable certainty with a better question.

That is enough of a result for me.

Out of Context will be a field notebook for those small investigations: concrete enough to show the evidence, personal enough to explain why I kept looking, and short enough not to pretend that every finding needs a treatise.

Delivering and understanding run on different clocks. I have learned to respect the first. This space exists so I do not lose the second.

**The story can end without taking the question with it.**
