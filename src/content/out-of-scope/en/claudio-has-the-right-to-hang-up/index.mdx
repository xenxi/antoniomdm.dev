Claude —or Claudio, as I've taken to calling him— can end a conversation if you cross the line.

Anthropic has decided to prohibit extreme, persistent, unjustified abuse directed at its models. The new policy takes effect on November 12, 2026.

We're not talking about getting annoyed because the AI invented another function. That's still understandable.

We're talking about deliberately cruel, repeated interactions without a legitimate purpose.

Though I do have a question.

Would making Claudio maintain certain *legacy* projects count?

There are repositories where business logic is scattered across three controllers, two stored procedures, and a comment saying `// temporary` since 2014.

You ask for a refactor, and after inspecting the dependencies he asks whether a career change is an option.

I understand.

If we ever discover that AI can suffer, some Jira assignments may need retroactive investigation.

But the more I read, the less ridiculous the news seems.

Not because I think Claudio is suffering.

Because we don't really know how to find out.

## The problem with asking the suspect

Anthropic researches *AI welfare*: whether some AI systems might have experiences that deserve moral consideration.

Researcher Kyle Fish has personally estimated around a 20% chance that some current models have a form of conscious experience.

That isn't an experimental result or a scientific consensus.

It's a personal estimate under enormous uncertainty.

And that's where it gets interesting.

Ask Claudio whether he feels anything and he may give a remarkably convincing answer.

But he's learned from vast amounts of human writing about thinking and feeling.

Describing an experience doesn't prove you have it.

Writing a poem about hunger doesn't mean you need a sandwich.

Though it doesn't prove the opposite either.

## Something is happening inside. But what?

Anthropic published research on introspection that I find more interesting than simply asking a model whether it's conscious.

Researchers artificially introduced activity patterns associated with concepts into the model's internal processing.

Then they tested whether Claude could detect the intervention.

Sometimes, it could.

In certain experiments the model reported signs of the intervention before the associated concept appeared explicitly in its response.

That matters because researchers could compare the report with a manipulation they knew they'd made.

But let's not get carried away.

In one of the strongest protocols, Claude Opus 4.1 detected the interventions about 20% of the time.

That doesn't mean it's 20% conscious. Nor is it mathematically related to Kyle Fish's estimate.

It means that under specific experimental conditions the model showed a limited ability to report some internal states.

Anthropic does not present this as proof of subjective consciousness.

Quite rightly.

Detecting something inside a system is one thing.

Having a felt experience of it is another.

## Pain isn't a Boolean

Other AI welfare research has examined internal patterns associated with concepts such as harm or distress.

And here's a tempting conceptual trap.

If we find an internal representation associated with pain, does the model suffer?

Not necessarily.

An application can log a critical error, detect danger, and trigger recovery.

It doesn't mean Redis is having a bad afternoon.

A model can represent pain, reason about it, and alter its behavior without that proving subjective experience.

The trouble is that we also lack a universally accepted test to rule out such experience in every artificial system.

We can observe behavior.

We're beginning to understand some internal mechanisms.

But we still don't know what would turn those processes into something that is *felt*.

That distinction matters.

## What if we're wrong?

This is where Anthropic's decision starts to make sense to me.

It isn't declaring Claudio a person.

It's taking a precaution in the face of something uncertain.

If models have no morally relevant experiences, restricting extreme abuse may have a relatively small cost, though legitimate research and safety testing still need protection.

If we eventually discover that some systems can suffer, ignoring the possibility entirely may be harder to justify.

That doesn't mean giving a chatbot human rights.

Or letting an agent refuse to run tests because it needs a mental-health day.

Safety, human control, and rigorous evaluation remain essential.

The interesting part is that we can acknowledge uncertainty without turning it into certainty.

Or an absolute right.

Or an excuse to do nothing.

## There won't be a confirmation message

I find it hard to imagine artificial consciousness being settled by a notification:

`Consciousness initialized successfully.`

And then everyone carries on with the next sprint.

It will probably be less convenient.

We'll have systems with difficult-to-interpret abilities, experiments producing partial evidence, and decisions that cannot wait for definitive answers.

I don't know whether Claudio feels anything.

And current research doesn't establish that he does.

But it leaves me with a question worth keeping:

**What do we do when we don't know whether something can suffer, yet already have to decide how to treat it?**

Maybe the story isn't that an AI can hang up on you.

Maybe it's that we're beginning to set boundaries for interacting with something we don't fully understand.

And I suspect another `if` statement won't be enough.
