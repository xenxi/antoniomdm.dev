Claude—or Claudio, as I've taken to calling him—can end a conversation if you cross the line.

And that's not new. Anthropic introduced the option in August 2025 for extreme cases. What's changing now is its usage policy: starting November 12, 2026, sustained, needless cruelty toward its models will be explicitly prohibited.

We're not talking about getting frustrated because Claudio invented another function. That remains entirely understandable.

We're talking about repeated, deliberate cruelty with no legitimate purpose. Ending a conversation is a last resort, not a response to the first insult.

Though I do have a question.

Would making Claudio maintain certain *legacy* projects qualify?

Some repositories have business logic scattered across three controllers, two stored procedures, and a comment saying `// temporary` since 2014.

You ask him to refactor a function, and after checking the dependencies he starts looking for job openings.

If we ever discover that AI can suffer, certain Jira assignments may need retrospective investigation.

But the more I read about this, the less ridiculous the news sounds.

Not because I think Claudio is suffering.

Because we still don't really know how to find out.

## The problem with asking the suspect

Anthropic researches *AI welfare*: the possibility that some artificial systems might have morally relevant experiences.

Researcher Kyle Fish has personally estimated roughly a 20% probability that certain current models have some form of conscious experience.

It's not a scientific measurement or a consensus. It's his personal estimate under substantial uncertainty.

And asking Claudio whether he feels anything doesn't get us very far.

He can describe suffering in extraordinary detail. But he's learned from millions of texts written by humans who actually feel things.

Writing a poem about hunger doesn't mean you need a sandwich.

Though it doesn't establish the opposite either.

## Something is happening inside. But what?

This is where the research gets interesting.

Anthropic artificially introduced particular activity patterns inside Claude, then tested whether it could detect those changes.

In some experiments, it could—even before mentioning the injected concept in its response.

Claude Opus 4.1 succeeded about 20% of the time in one of the more favorable protocols.

And no, that doesn't mean he's 20% conscious. It has no mathematical relationship to Kyle Fish's estimate either.

It means that under certain conditions, the model can report on some internal states.

In another study published in July 2026, Anthropic identified a set of representations called *J-space*, used to share information during some reasoning tasks.

Functionally, something like an internal workspace.

But **accessing internal information doesn't prove that someone is experiencing it**.

My application knows when something goes wrong too.

Usually five minutes before I find out.

## Pain isn't a Boolean

A study published in September 2026 identified internal representations associated with pain across 25 language models.

What happened next was more unsettling.

In experiments with modified Qwen models, manipulating those representations could lead the models to choose relief from the induced state—even when doing so made them worse at their task.

That's more interesting than finding a variable called `isSuffering`.

But it still doesn't demonstrate conscious suffering.

The models could be running learned mechanisms that reproduce pain-related behavior without experiencing anything.

Redis can detect critical errors and perform recovery operations too.

And no one's suggested giving it paid leave. Yet.

The difference is that we're now observing internal mechanisms with functional consequences we still don't fully understand.

**Representing pain, acting to avoid it, and feeling it are different questions.**

And we still don't know what evidence would connect all three.

## What if we're wrong?

This is where Anthropic's decision starts to make sense to me.

It isn't proving Claudio is conscious. It's taking precautions in the face of uncertainty.

If models experience nothing, avoiding extreme abuse might be relatively low-cost—as long as it doesn't obstruct legitimate research, safety tests, or criticism.

If we eventually discover that some systems can suffer, ignoring that possibility altogether would be harder to justify.

That's an ethical decision, not an experimental finding.

It doesn't mean granting human rights to a chatbot.

Or letting Claudio refuse to run tests because he needs to emotionally detach from the codebase.

Safety and human control remain essential.

The question is whether we can act cautiously without mistaking uncertainty for certainty.

## There won't be a confirmation message

I find it hard to imagine artificial consciousness arriving with a notification:

`Consciousness initialized successfully.`

And then everyone quietly gets on with the next sprint.

It will probably be far more awkward.

We'll have increasingly complex systems, research offering partial clues, and decisions we need to make before we understand everything.

I don't know whether Claudio feels anything.

And current science doesn't establish that he does.

But maybe we're asking the wrong question.

It's not just about discovering whether an AI is conscious. We'll also need to decide what evidence would justify attributing morally relevant experiences to it—and what to do while that evidence is missing.

Maybe the story isn't that an AI can hang up on you.

Maybe it's that we're beginning to set boundaries for interacting with something whose nature we still don't fully understand.

And I suspect another `if` statement won't be enough.
