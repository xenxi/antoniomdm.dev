The idea sounded simple enough: turn my portfolio into something resembling an operating system.

A desktop, draggable windows, a dock, a terminal, even an Arcade mode.

Because apparently showing four projects in a grid was far too conventional.

And so AntoñiOS was born.

The interesting part came when I started thinking about something much less exciting than windows: **what should happen when someone opens a direct link?**

You can build the prettiest desktop in the world, but if someone arrives from Google looking for a project and gets a welcome screen instead, something might need another look.

## A desktop isn't a website

On an operating system, the desktop is usually where you start.

On the web, it isn't.

Someone can arrive directly at `/projects/`, `/architecture/`, or a blog post.

They expect to find what they came for.

Not a startup animation.

Not an empty window.

And preferably not a button saying “Click here to begin.”

AntoñiOS had to reconcile two kinds of navigation.

On one side, the visual experience: opening apps, minimizing windows, switching focus, and moving around the desktop.

On the other, the browser's usual behavior: shareable URLs, history, the Back button, and pages that still work after a reload.

The current solution combines Astro for static pages with Preact for the interactive layer.

Two worlds that can coexist perfectly well.

As long as neither tries to take over the other.

## Window state is not the URL

The window manager has its own state.

It knows which apps are open, which one has focus, which are minimized, and their stacking order.

It also manages positions and sizes.

Nothing revolutionary.

Until you connect it to navigation.

Imagine I open Projects and then Architecture.

Architecture is now in front.

I press Back.

What should happen?

The browser wants to return to the previous route. The desktop still has its own open windows.

If they operate independently, the URL can say one thing while the interface shows another.

A frontend multiverse.

And we have enough continuity problems without adding interdimensional travel.

In AntoñiOS, desktop actions update the active route, and browser navigation can restore the corresponding app.

History isn't decoration.

It's part of the contract.

## It also has to work when the magic disappears

There's another detail I care about.

An interface can be very convincing when everything works exactly as expected.

But what if JavaScript isn't available?

What if someone navigates by keyboard?

What if they open the site on a phone, where dragging floating windows makes very little sense?

AntoñiOS generates HTML for its routes at build time. The main content doesn't depend entirely on the interactive desktop running.

On small screens, apps stop behaving like free-floating windows and use the available space.

There isn't much point recreating Windows on a six-inch screen.

Sometimes adapting an experience means giving part of it up.

That's harder to accept than adding another animation.

## Not everything needs to survive F5

Another interesting decision concerns persistence.

AntoñiOS remembers some preferences: wallpaper, effects, and sound.

But it doesn't preserve every window's position and dimensions indefinitely.

That feels like a sensible distinction.

Remembering how I like my desktop is one thing. Turning every movement into data that must survive future visits is another.

Persistence always sounds easy.

Until you need to decide when data expires, how to restore it, or what happens when the device changes.

Suddenly we have a synchronization problem just to remember that yesterday I left a window twenty pixels farther to the right.

Some architectural battles are worth winning.

Others are worth not starting.

## The interface was the excuse

What interests me most about AntoñiOS isn't making a website look like an operating system.

It's how many decisions appear when you try to do that without losing what makes the web useful.

Real routes, accessible content, predictable navigation, clearly bounded state.

The desktop is the presentation.

The architecture still has to work when you remove the scenery.

Maybe that's the lesson I take from the project: **a good experience isn't about adding every possible interaction, but choosing which ones are worth complicating the system for**.

Because building a fictional operating system is fun.

Explaining why the Back button stopped working is considerably less so.
