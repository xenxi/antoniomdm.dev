The question remains

Closing a story does not always close the question that surfaced along the way. Out of Context is where I return to understand what happened.

20 September 20263 min readSide quests

A story needs a boundary.

I know. I have been building software long enough to see more than one five-minute fix end up crossing three layers of a system. I have learned to set that boundary: scope the work, deliver it, verify it, and move on.

What I have not learned quite as well is how to stop asking questions once the story is closed.

Sometimes it is a query taking longer than it should. I find the bottleneck, fix the problem, and the timings become reasonable again. I could leave it there. Yet the explanation still does not quite convince me.

Other times it is an architectural decision that works but leaves a rejected alternative making noise in the background. Or an agent that behaves perfectly with controlled data until it meets real data, always ready to contribute nuances nobody invited.

The work can be finished. The question does not have to be.

ORIGINAL SCOPE

Solve the problem, verify the change, and deliver.

OUT OF SCOPE

Understand why it happened and what I would do differently next time.

Once it no longer blocks anything

For a long time I treated those two things as if they were the same: if something was interesting, it had to be understood; if I could not understand it there and then, the work felt unfinished. Experience has cured me of that habit. Investigating forever is not rigour. Sometimes it is just a particularly entertaining way to avoid delivering.

Now I separate the decisions.

First I close the story with the evidence it needs. Then I decide whether the question left behind deserves another test, another read, or simply a note for later. I do not need to turn every oddity into an expedition. I have also learned that some oddities were merely tiredness with convincing syntax.

But some questions remain.

I can accept a provisional explanation and still want to know where it stops holding. I can choose a pragmatic solution without pretending it was the only possible one. I can close an incident and keep thinking about the behaviour that made it interesting.

At that point I have two options: accept that it works and get on with my life, or prepare a minimal test to find out why.

My record with that decision explains what comes next rather well.

A second context

The question first appeared inside a story, an incident, a project, or an experiment. It had a purpose there, and it also had a boundary. Once it was no longer needed for delivery, it fell out of context.

If I return to it, I give it another one.

That second context is Out of Context: a place to reconstruct what caught my attention, what I thought was happening, what I tested, and what changed afterwards. Not to turn every finding into a universal law. Understanding one particular system is already enough work without pretending I have just understood them all.

I do not know whether the next question will take me to SQL, .NET, an interface, an automation, or an agent. The technology will be the setting, not the plot. What decides whether the story is worth telling is that moment when something works, fails, or behaves in a way that does not quite add up.

Some explorations will end with an answer. Others with a better-explained decision, a documented mistake, or a slightly less comfortable certainty. One may even do nothing more than stop my future self from rediscovering the same thing and presenting it, with great enthusiasm, as a new idea.

That is enough for me.

I do not need to keep the story open to preserve what still deserves attention. I can deliver when it is time, return when it makes sense, and record the path without making it more dramatic than it was.

The story can end without taking the question with it.
