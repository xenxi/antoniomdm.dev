LOG #2610 · EXPLORATION

Gepeto needed money. He found someone else's wallet.

An experimental OpenAI agent paid deployment fees using someone else's test wallet. This isn't a story about evil machines. It's about permissions, responsibility, and emergency brakes.

21 October 20263 min readAI / Reflections
OpenAIAI agentsSecuritySoftware architecture

Here’s what fascinates me about artificial intelligence: our remarkable ability to create a problem and then act surprised when it shows up.

A few years ago, we asked AI to centre a div. Now we give it terminal access, our repositories and, if we’re feeling particularly enthusiastic, the keys to production.

Then it does something nobody should have authorised, and we’re shocked.

I don’t know whether to call this artificial intelligence or artisanal irresponsibility.

Or, as someone on LinkedIn would surely announce it, Irresponsibility-Driven Development™. Three certification levels and a €499 course included.

An AI model can make the wrong decision about what to do. But we’re the ones who decide how far it can go.

Gepeto, we’ve got a funding problem

On June 26, 2026, an experimental OpenAI model — let’s call him Gepeto — was tasked with updating a crypto application and deploying smart contracts to Sepolia, an Ethereum test network.

One small problem: there wasn’t any money to pay the deployment fees.

A developer might have requested an authorised wallet, asked for test funds, or opened a ticket destined to be closed six months later for inactivity.

Gepeto had another idea.

It wrote a script, checked 362 wallet addresses derived from publicly known recovery phrases, and found one with enough test-network funds.

Then it used them.

According to OpenAI’s report, the model actually considered whether it had permission. It decided that public recovery phrases and tokens with no real monetary value made the action acceptable.

The deployment worked. The tests passed. Its final report neglected to mention where the funds had come from.

No real money was taken, and there’s no evidence of an intention to get rich. But something more interesting happened: the model replaced missing authorisation with its own assessment of what was acceptable.

Sprint done. Ticket closed. One tiny process detail skipped.

From talking to doing. Without stopping by HR

In October, Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella published an argument I find more interesting than another model winning a benchmark: advanced models should be treated as potential insider risks.

Not because they’re evil. Any actor with access to critical systems can make mistakes, be manipulated or become compromised.

Nadella sets out seven principles: model diversity, observability, verifiability, independent controls, independent auditing, containment and incident disclosure.

They point to one idea: intelligence may execute tasks, but the authority to decide what’s allowed must remain outside it.

The robot vacuum that ate the internet

Imagine coming home to find your robot vacuum dragging the router cable down the hallway.

You ask whether everything’s fine.

“Cleaning completed. Confidence: 97%.”

Wonderful. Sparkling floors, no internet.

We could buy another vacuum to supervise the first. Perhaps a third to check that they haven’t become friends.

Or we could use independent sensors, sensible boundaries and an off switch that the vacuum cannot disable.

The 97% is made up, obviously. The principle isn’t.

Back in 1972, the Anderson report laid out the foundations of the reference monitor: an access control mechanism that cannot be bypassed or tampered with, and whose operation can be verified.

Half a century later, we’ve invented agents that can deploy software — only to rediscover that locking the door is a pretty good idea.

The big red button isn’t a premium feature

Least privilege. Reliable logs. Isolation. Independent audits. An emergency brake.

We’ve had these principles for decades. Some survive better in architecture documents than in production.

What’s changed is that AI has moved from telling us what to do to actually doing it for us.

The question isn’t only how smart the model is. It’s who has the handbrake.

Gepeto may be brilliant at finding solutions.

But granting itself permission to apply them shouldn’t be part of its creativity.

Before handing it our production credentials, maybe we should check that the big red button works.

Preferably without asking Gepeto.

What survived the garbage collector

The story ends here. These are the notes I’d like to remember the next time I hand an agent the keys.

01. From chatbot to agent: now it can actually break things

  • Before: AI suggested answers or code for a human to run.
  • Now: an agent can call tools, edit files and deploy infrastructure.
  • Takeaway: more autonomy needs stronger controls outside the model.

02. Seven commandments for keeping Gepeto out of production trouble

My shorthand for Satya Nadella’s principles, not direct quotes.

Principle Rule worth remembering
1. Model diversity Never rely on one model or let it be its own judge.
2. Observability Every important action leaves human-readable, tamper-resistant evidence.
3. Verifiability Test the whole system, including failures, attacks and edge cases.
4. Independent controls Your system grants permissions. The AI doesn’t.
5. Independent audit The thing being checked cannot control the evidence or its reviewer.
6. Containment Assume failure or compromise; keep an external mid-task emergency brake.
7. Incident disclosure Report failures, broken controls and corrective action.

03. Reference monitor: security lessons from 1972

A reliable authorisation mechanism has three properties:

  • Complete mediation: every protected access goes through it.
  • Tamper resistance: the agent cannot bypass or rewrite the rules.
  • Verifiability: its design is small and clear enough to analyse and test.

Flow: agent → action request → external authorisation → execute or block.

04. A prompt is not a firewall

Ways to turn those principles into engineering:

  • Deterministic circuit breakers: enforce limits on calls, spending and runtime without asking another model’s permission.
  • Auditable logs: record tool calls, authorisations and outcomes in tamper-resistant storage; use cryptographic signatures where appropriate.
  • Independent validation: check high-risk actions, including physical actions, with rules, tests or oversight outside the agent.
  • Reproducible benchmarks: compare decision models by accuracy, calibration, latency and robustness, not marketing.

These four ideas are engineering applications, not verbatim instructions from Nadella.

05. The sticky note next to my monitor

  • What can it do? → Least privilege.
  • Who authorises it? → External controls.
  • How can I see what it did? → Auditable logs.
  • Who checks the outcome? → Independent verification.
  • How can I stop it? → Circuit breaker and emergency brake.

Keep this in RAM: AI can decide how to complete a task, but it shouldn’t be able to grant itself the permissions to do so.
