LOG #2610 · EXPLORATION

The price was right. Yesterday.

A cache can improve latency while returning stale information. Between Redis and business rules, how long can we afford a value to be wrong?

17 October 20264 min readArchitecture
Architecture.NETRedisCaching

There’s a remarkably efficient way to return the wrong price: put it in Redis.

It’s fast. It scales. The metrics look fantastic.

Unfortunately, the customer still expects the right price. Some people are impossible to please.

I’ve worked with pricing, availability, and business rules long enough to be suspicious of optimizations that look too easy.

And few things look easier than adding a cache.

Until you ask what you’re actually caching.

A price never travels alone

Picture a commercial system.

There’s a product, several dealers, campaigns, discounts, taxes, and a few conditions the business considers perfectly reasonable.

We want faster queries.

The obvious answer: calculate the price once, store it, and skip the repeated work.

price:product:123 → 249.99

Beautiful.

Except the price may depend on the dealer, campaign, date, or customer conditions.

A minor detail.

The cache isn’t to blame. We asked it to remember an answer without telling it the question.

We can improve the key, include context, and version the data. Then another question appears.

How long is that answer still valid?

Five minutes is not a strategy

One of my favorite moments in cache design is when TTL comes up.

—How long should we keep it?

—Five minutes.

—Why?

—Well… five minutes sounds fine, doesn’t it?

And just like that, we’ve made a business decision in a configuration file.

Five minutes might be harmless for a product description and far too long for a price that’s just changed.

Microsoft’s guidance on the cache-aside pattern makes an important point: the cache does not guarantee consistency with the source.

And Redis, however fast it is, cannot decide how long we’re willing to show stale information.

That’s our job.

Invalidating an entry after updating the source doesn’t magically remove every race condition, either. Another read can get ahead, hit a lagging replica, or repopulate the cache with an old value.

A TTL doesn’t solve those problems.

It just puts an expiration date on our confidence.

The question that changes the design

Here’s a thought experiment I find useful.

A promotion ends at noon.

A customer checks the price at 11:59.

At 12:01, the page is still open.

At 12:02, they try to confirm the purchase.

What should happen?

There is no universal answer.

We might allow slightly stale prices on screen, recalculate before confirmation, or guarantee an offer for a defined period.

Different decisions. Different designs.

None is settled by choosing Redis, MemoryCache, or the latest caching library for .NET.

They’re settled by understanding the business contract.

The important question isn’t how long a value stays cached. It’s how long we can afford it to be wrong.

Performance can lie, too

I like metrics.

Latency, percentiles, hit rates, database load. They all matter.

But a 99% cache-hit rate doesn’t necessarily mean the system is doing its job well.

It might mean we’re returning stale data with admirable efficiency.

For commercially sensitive data, I also want to know about invalidations, data age, and discrepancies discovered when operations are recalculated.

We don’t always need immediate consistency. Sometimes accepting briefly stale data is exactly the right trade-off.

What matters is making that decision deliberately.

And documenting it.

Preferably before someone discovers the behavior in production.

Redis wasn’t the problem

We have increasingly good tools for implementing caches. We can even ask an AI agent to set up the integration, keys, and expiration policies.

It will probably do it quickly.

But no library can answer this for us:

“What happens if this value stops being correct?”

That’s the conversation worth having before optimizing.

Because a cache can dramatically reduce latency.

It can also deliver the wrong price in record time.

And however good the performance looks, that shouldn’t count as a win.
