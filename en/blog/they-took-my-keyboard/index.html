They took my keyboard. They left me the decisions.

GitHub migrated the Copilot runtime to Rust with AI agents. The interesting part is not the 800,000 lines of code: it is how a developer's time, judgement, and value are changing.

22 October 20265 min readAgentic engineering
Software engineeringAIAgentsCareer development

Agents are writing more and more code. We’re still responsible for deciding what to build, how to build it, and—above all—why.

I’ve been developing software for more than fifteen years.

I’ve learned languages, frameworks, design patterns, and enough ways to centre a div to question a few of my life choices.

And now an AI can write in minutes code that used to take me hours.

Wonderful.

So what am I supposed to do?

Until fairly recently, my job description seemed straightforward enough. Someone gave me a problem, I designed a solution, and I wrote code until it worked. Then we discovered the problem was something else, the requirements changed, and we started again.

Software development, in other words.

But after reading how GitHub migrated the Copilot runtime from TypeScript to Rust, I think we’re facing a much deeper change than writing code faster.

Where we spend our time is changing. And with it, where we create value.

800,000 lines and one guy asking questions

Stephen Toub led a migration that produced more than 800,000 lines of production Rust.

128 pull requests. About three and a half months. Agents writing most of the code.

A project he estimates would previously have required a team for one or two years was completed largely under one developer’s direction, with important contributions from the rest of the team.

We already covered the migration’s problems in “The PR was green. The problem was still there.”.

But there’s another detail I find even more interesting.

Toub analysed the 2,639 messages he had sent to the agents, classifying them by intent.

  • 31%: reviews, testing, and continuous integration.
  • 17.4%: challenging technical and design decisions.
  • 15%: pushing to get the job properly finished.

Those three activities accounted for 63% of his interactions.

These are percentages of messages classified with Copilot, not an exact record of hours worked. But they tell us something.

Most of his interventions weren’t explanations of how to write a function.

They were about checking, challenging, and deciding.

The work didn’t disappear. It moved.

Instead of building every component himself, he was directing a system capable of building them.

A developer turned orchestra conductor, basically.

Except the musicians improvise, someone invents a new score, and everyone insists the tests pass.

Manufacturing nonsense at industrial speed

For years, implementation has consumed a substantial part of our working time.

That had a curious side effect: building things was expensive enough that, occasionally, we had to ask whether they were worth building.

Now we can create a prototype in an afternoon.

Or five.

Or ask Gepeto to design a microservices architecture for an application that stores three preferences and a username.

With Kubernetes, naturally. We’re professionals.

The problem is that making something cheaper does not make it necessary.

If implementation once consumed 70% of our effort, and we can drastically reduce that share, we have an extraordinary opportunity.

We can understand the business better, investigate alternatives, try risky ideas, simplify systems, and tackle the problems we always postponed.

Or we can fill the world with features nobody asked for, nobody needs, and somebody will have to maintain.

Production speed is no longer necessarily our main constraint.

Choosing wisely—and knowing what to reject—starts to matter more.

Rejecting one bad idea can create more value than implementing ten good ones.

Although it’s rather harder to explain in a sprint retrospective.

Knowing how to code matters. Perhaps more than ever.

Here’s the paradox.

If agents write more and more code, do we still need to learn programming?

I think so. Very much so.

But perhaps we need to rethink what we’re learning it for.

An agent can propose an architecture. I need to understand architecture to judge it.

It can optimise a query. I need to understand performance to know whether anything improved.

It can introduce concurrency, caching, events, and seventeen layers of abstraction.

I need experience to stop it in time. Possibly a fire extinguisher.

Delegating execution does not mean delegating judgement.

In fact, the more solutions we can generate, the more important it becomes to distinguish good ones from those that merely look good.

And that raises another uncomfortable question.

How does a junior developer gain experience if AI keeps solving the problems that used to teach us?

I don’t have a definitive answer, but I suspect we’ll need to separate two activities that used to go hand in hand: producing and learning.

In production, we’ll use the tools that deliver the best results.

To learn, we may need to keep solving certain problems ourselves, taking solutions apart, experimenting, and understanding why they work.

Like using a calculator: an excellent tool, provided you can spot when it says splitting the restaurant bill four ways will cost €800 per person.

The goal shouldn’t be to know less because the machine knows more.

It should be to use the machine to go further with what we know.

So what makes a good developer now?

I think our ability to move between three responsibilities will matter increasingly.

The first is understanding the problem.

Not merely receiving requirements, but understanding the context, constraints, and consequences of our decisions.

The second is directing the solution.

Choosing strategies, breaking down problems, setting boundaries, experimenting, and coordinating tools and agents without losing sight of the bigger picture.

The third is owning the outcome.

Not just ensuring the code works, but that it solves the right problem, remains sustainable, and makes sense to the people using it.

None of these responsibilities is new.

What’s new is that, as we automate much of implementation, they may become the centre of our working day.

That also changes how we should measure our work.

A developer who prevents an unnecessary feature may create more value than someone who generates twenty pull requests.

A team that simplifies a system may achieve more than one that doubles its output.

And someone who discovers that a process change solves the problem—without touching a single line of code—may have done the best work of all.

Even if they then have to explain in Jira what they were doing all week.

And when agents start making the decisions too?

Because that’s the next step.

Today agents implement tasks, run tests, and propose solutions.

It’s reasonable to think they’ll gradually take on more complete jobs: from analysing a need to developing, validating, and maintaining a solution.

Perhaps we’ll see smaller teams tackling far more ambitious projects. Or individual professionals building products that previously required a substantial organisation.

That possibility excites me.

But I don’t think it automatically means developers will disappear, or that we’ll all turn into machine supervisors.

Some decisions will be automated too. New specialisations will emerge. Teams will change and, quite probably, some of today’s work will lose economic value.

We don’t know how much, or how quickly.

And that’s the part I find genuinely interesting.

If tools keep taking on what we currently call our job, we’ll have to keep discovering where we can contribute more value.

That requires continuous learning, but also understanding products, business, costs, risks, and people.

Because engineering has never been merely about finding a technically possible solution.

It’s about finding one worth pursuing.

The keyboard was never the point

I admit there’s a little nostalgia involved.

I enjoy programming. I like solving a hard problem, working out why something fails, and finding an elegant solution.

I wouldn’t want that part of the job to disappear.

But I also don’t want to spend two days writing code a tool can produce in ten minutes just to feel like I’m still a developer.

I’d rather invest that time in building something I couldn’t previously afford to attempt.

In learning something new.

Or in discovering that we’ve spent three sprints perfectly solving a problem nobody had.

Maybe we’ll write far less code in a few years. Maybe our days will look more like directing projects, making decisions, and experimenting with ideas.

I don’t know exactly what it will look like.

What I do believe is that we’ve spent too long confusing our tool with our profession.

We’re not paid to write code. We’re paid to solve problems using our expertise.

And now we have tools capable of multiplying what we can do with that expertise.

The question is no longer how much code we can produce.

It’s what we’re going to do with that capability.

Although, please, let us keep coding occasionally.

I didn’t survive fifteen years of JavaScript just to end up working exclusively in PowerPoint.

What I’m taking into the next sprint

  • Output isn’t the same as value. Just because something is easier or cheaper to produce doesn’t mean it’s worth producing.
  • Judgement moves centre stage. The more solutions we can generate, the more important it is to choose, challenge, and reject wisely.
  • Delegation doesn’t replace learning. Automation frees up time, but knowledge remains essential to making good decisions.
  • Work is changing, not just accelerating. Automating tasks changes responsibilities, skills, and how we measure results.
  • The future doesn’t come with a fixed roadmap. Adapting means understanding fundamentals and continuing to learn, not chasing every new tool.

To keep in RAM: the goal isn’t to compete with AI at writing code faster. It’s to use it to solve problems that used to be beyond our reach.
