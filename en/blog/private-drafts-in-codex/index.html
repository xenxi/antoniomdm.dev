LOG #2611 · EXPLORATION

They put their drafts into Codex. OpenAI arrived with ten thousand agents.

Two mathematicians trusted AI tools with unpublished research. When one of those companies announced a related breakthrough, a deeply awkward question followed: had their data been used?

2 November 20263 min readDrama with Receipts
AICodexResearchPrivacy

Tristan Buckmaster and Levent Alpöge had spent months researching with help from Claude and Codex. They had fed their project drafts into Codex.

Then OpenAI deployed around 10,000 agents and announced a solution to the Navier–Stokes Millennium Prize problem.

The timing was positively cinematic.

Evidence that anyone copied their work, however, has not emerged.

Was the copilot also playing for the other team?

The mathematicians were studying a related question about the Euler equations. They were building on a research programme pioneered by Diego Córdoba and Luis Martínez-Zoroa.

OpenAI acknowledges hearing rumours of mathematical progress on September 1 and launching its own agent effort.

Buckmaster says word about their unpublished research had reached the company.

So he asked directly whether its models had accessed their private Codex sessions.

According to his account, he was told the model did not look up user data. When he pressed about whether that data could have been used for training, he received no answer.

Having an AI help with your homework is one thing. Discovering its owner is racing to hand in the assignment first is something else entirely.

Public relations would like a fire extinguisher

Things became even messier during discussions about authorship.

Buckmaster says he was offered the chance to present an OpenAI result on his own, without Alpöge, who works at Anthropic.

And when he said he would go public, he says he heard: “Why would you ruin your career?”

OpenAI researcher Sébastien Bubeck disputes that interpretation. He said they were discussing authorship of an OpenAI paper, not stripping Alpöge of credit for the pair’s own research. Bubeck apologised for his words.

About those drafts. Here’s the awkward bit.

OpenAI initially said it could not entirely rule out an indirect influence from de-identified product-use data on model improvements.

On September 10, following an internal investigation, it said Buckmaster’s Codex prompts from the preceding two months could not have influenced its result, including through training.

Crucially, Buckmaster says he does not know whether his data was used and is not directly accusing anyone of theft.

There is no public proof of such theft.

What bothers me is a different architectural problem: the company supplying the tool where you store unpublished research may also become your competitor.

Maybe nobody took the homework. But handing the notebook to the other team is starting to look like a questionable design decision.

What survived the fire

  • Suspicion is not evidence. A striking coincidence and a vague reply justify scrutiny, not a verdict.
  • Data is a competitive asset. Before sharing unpublished work, understand access, retention and training policies rather than assuming the boundaries.
  • Trust needs controls. Data separation, auditing and traceability should exist before the inevitable controversy forces everyone to ask for them.

Save to RAM: when your tool provider could also be your competitor, know exactly what you’re sharing.
